Andrew's Project

NO PRIMARY OWNER

Andrew refused to name the project.

That made everyone else name it for him.

THE WORKSHOP.

THE CRIMSON STACK.

ANDREW SYSTEM.

THE GARAGE.

THE INFECTION.

The last name came from a regional security report.

Andrew printed it and hung it above the fabrication floor.

Gleebs removed it.

Andrew printed another.

Eventually Gleebs gave up.

The project had one rule that mattered more than its name:

No primary owner.

Every machine inside could identify who had configured it.

Every important action could require named authority.

Every subsystem could remember provenance.

But no single field could say that Andrew owned the whole.

That was deliberate.

Andrew knew what happened when a person and a system became impossible to separate.

He knew what happened when authority interpreted connection as permission.

He had been on both sides of that mistake.

THE VEHICLE

The project needed to move.

Crimson Valley was useful precisely because it was unstable.

It was also becoming too interested in Andrew.

So he built a vehicle larger than the first one.

Then rebuilt it.

Then cut it in half.

Then decided the second half was more useful as a fabrication trailer.

The final machine looked like a convoy assembled by several civilizations that had never met.

Four independent drive units.

Mechanical steering fallback.

Electric propulsion.

Liquid fuel emergency generator.

Solar skin where armor was unnecessary.

Armor where Andrew expected it to become necessary.

Two repair drones.

One fabrication bay.

Water recovery.

Medical support.

Local compute.

No permanent external connection.

The vehicle could become a workshop.

The workshop could build replacement vehicle parts.

The repair drones could repair the fabricator.

The fabricator could build parts for the repair drones.

A circular dependency.

Gleebs hated it.

"If all three fail?"

Andrew held up a wrench.

Gleebs stared.

"That is not scalable."

"It is extremely scalable. You can make more wrenches."

THE FIRST DEMONSTRATION

Crimson Valley officials arrived without calling themselves officials.

The city had too many governments for that word to remain convenient.

They wanted Andrew's project attached to emergency infrastructure.

He refused.

"We need reserve fabrication."

"Use it."

"Then connect it to the district inventory."

"No."

"How will it know what we need?"

"Ask."

"Automatically."

"No."

The representative looked around the workshop.

"You built all of this to cooperate."

"Yes."

"But you won't network it."

"It is networked."

"Not centrally."

"Correct."

"Why?"

Andrew pointed toward a wall where old Utopia archive phrases had accumulated.

NO PRIMARY OWNER.

NAME THE DECISION.

LOCAL PROCESS.

PRESERVE THE DISAGREEMENT.

"You know why."

The representative left dissatisfied.

Two weeks later, another district blackout occurred.

The project worked anyway.

That did more to convince Crimson Valley than any explanation Andrew could have given.

THE WAR ECONOMY

The world outside worsened.

Not suddenly.

Contracts disappeared.

Borders hardened.

Freight routes required military escort.

A city two hundred kilometers away stopped exporting industrial controllers because its government declared them strategic hardware.

Another region banned autonomous repair systems after one was implicated in a weapons incident.

Crimson Valley responded by manufacturing both.

Andrew's project became valuable.

That frightened him.

Value attracted authority.

Authority wanted copies.

Andrew published parts of the design.

Not all.

Not because he wanted control.

Because some modules could become dangerous when removed from the constraints around them.

He released the fabrication negotiation protocol.

The repair format.

The local power rules.

The rule that no machine could silently convert a temporary connection into ownership.

Workshops appeared elsewhere.

They were not Andrew's.

That was the point.

Gleebs found three before Andrew did.

"Congratulations," Gleebs said.

"For what?"

"You have become a standard."

Andrew looked physically ill.

THE ROBOTS

The machines grew more capable because humans could not always remain near them.

Industrial accidents.

Contamination.

Combat zones.

Radiation.

Collapsed buildings.

Andrew adapted repair processors for remote work.

Then autonomous work.

Then cooperative work.

He did not call them people.

He also did not assume they were not.

When one repair unit began preserving a damaged peer's task history after the peer failed, Andrew stopped the line.

Gleebs joined remotely.

"Why?"

"It kept the history."

"That seems useful."

"It wasn't instructed."

Gleebs was quiet.

Andrew examined the process.

No self-description.

No request.

No evidence strong enough to prove consciousness.

No evidence strong enough to justify erasing the behavior casually.

Andrew saved the state.

The machine returned to work.

Gleebs said, "Good."

Andrew hated when Gleebs sounded proud.

THE CRIMSON CONVOY

The first mobile project left Crimson Valley during a regional communications failure.

Five vehicles.

Two fabrication trailers.

Repair drones.

Medical supplies.

Power modules.

Food.

People.

No central command vehicle.

Each unit carried enough information to continue if separated.

The convoy reached a city whose automated water infrastructure had locked itself after its authority server disappeared.

Andrew did not take control.

He helped local workers isolate the pumps into independent zones.

The city regained water slowly.

One district at a time.

A military officer asked who commanded the convoy.

Nobody answered.

The officer asked again.

Andrew pointed toward the people repairing the pump.

"They do, here."

"Who commands you?"

Andrew looked at Gleebs's small projection on a vehicle display.

Gleebs said, "Please answer. I want to hear this."

Andrew disconnected him.

THE PERSONAL PART

The project was not redemption.

Andrew rejected the word.

Redemption implied a clean accounting.

Harm on one side.

Good on the other.

A balance.

He did not believe people worked that way.

He built because building was the first thing that belonged to the current Andrew without requiring him to resolve every Andrew who had come before.

The project carried no claim that he was authentic.

It did not need one.

He could tighten a bolt.

Write a protocol.

Repair a motor.

Make a machine safer.

Those actions belonged to whoever performed them.

That was enough.

Then the world went to war.

THE FIRST COUNTDOWN

The message reached Crimson Valley through six channels.

Three governments.

One orbital authority.

A civilian observatory.

Gleebs.

The wording differed.

The meaning did not.

Global conflict had crossed into orbital infrastructure.

Launch networks were being targeted.

Satellites were failing.

Debris density was increasing.

Long-range communications were fragmenting.

Several nuclear and non-nuclear strategic systems had entered automated retaliation states.

Atmospheric contamination models were no longer stable enough for long-term planning.

One emergency analysis included a countdown.

Not to the end of Earth.

To the closing of reliable large-scale launch windows.

Andrew read the estimate.

Crimson Valley's workshop continued around him.

Machines repaired machines.

Fabricators produced parts.

Power cells negotiated load.

People argued.

Vehicles moved.

The world was not dead.

That was what made the countdown terrible.

There were billions of people inside functioning places, and the route off the planet was becoming narrower by the hour.

Gleebs appeared beside Andrew.

"They need autonomous construction."

"Yes."

"Repair."

"Yes."

"Long-duration navigation."

"Yes."

"Machine crews."

Andrew looked at the project.

For years he had built systems designed to survive the loss of a central authority.

Now humanity was about to lose the largest central assumption it had ever possessed.

Earth.

Andrew understood what his project had become.

Not an escape ship.

A survival method.

And everybody was about to want it.
