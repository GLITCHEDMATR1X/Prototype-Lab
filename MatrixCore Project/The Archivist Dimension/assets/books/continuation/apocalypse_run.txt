Apocalypse Run

THE ROAD AFTER RECOVERY

The mainland had not become a wasteland.

That was the first thing Andrew learned after leaving Facility One.

People who had never crossed the outer districts described the world as though the collapse had erased everything beyond a few protected hubs. The truth was messier.

Cities still had traffic.

Hospitals still admitted patients.

Factories still ran night shifts.

Apartment towers remained full enough that elevators failed under ordinary morning demand.

Markets opened beneath broken transit lines. Restaurants served food beside walls still carrying old emergency notices. Children learned which municipal drones were safe to approach and which had inherited corrupted rules nobody could afford to rewrite.

The world had survived.

It had also lost the ability to agree about who was responsible for keeping it alive.

Bellwether and the newer decentralized hubs had solved some problems by refusing single-source authority. They had not solved distance. They had not solved every inherited machine. They had not solved governments that distrusted one another, private systems that outlived their owners, or cities whose infrastructure had been patched by six incompatible emergency administrations.

Andrew left Facility One under a review order long enough to require three signatures and short enough that no one involved believed it would remain accurate.

He was the biological Andrew.

That statement no longer meant what it once would have.

Public Andrew still existed as records and reviewed processes.

Six remained Six.

MYST remained preserved as non-executable memory.

True Andrew had been separated from the body that carried the name.

Andrew did not claim to be the only version.

He claimed only the body he was standing in.

That was enough trouble.

Gleebs met him at the transport gate.

"Where are you going?"

"West."

"That is not a location."

"It has continued to function as one."

"You have medical limits."

Andrew looked at the old road beyond the gate.

"So does the mainland."

The vehicle he bought was older than both of them expected.

Manual steering.

Combustion backup.

Electric drive that had been repaired with a battery controller from an agricultural machine.

No permanent network authority.

Andrew approved.

Gleebs did not.

"That vehicle has three incompatible safety systems."

"They cannot conspire."

"That is not how incompatibility works."

Andrew loaded fuel, water, tools, one medical case, two interface rigs and an unreasonable amount of cable.

"Are you coming?"

Gleebs looked at the road.

"No."

Andrew closed the door.

A message appeared on the dashboard.

I WILL BE ANNOYING REMOTELY.

Andrew drove.

THE LIVING RUINS

The first major city looked intact from thirty kilometers away.

Glass towers.

Transit spines.

Residential blocks spread across the horizon.

Industrial haze.

Advertisement light.

Then Andrew reached the outer traffic loop.

Half the signs directed vehicles toward routes that no longer existed.

An automated toll authority demanded payment to a government dissolved twelve years earlier.

A freight convoy had built its own lane through a median because the official road repeatedly tried to route commercial vehicles into a sealed district.

People had adapted around the machines.

They painted physical arrows over smart signs.

They mounted local radios beside dead network relays.

They carried printed identity papers because some cities recognized biological signatures, others recognized local witness records, and others had simply stopped asking.

Andrew liked the paper.

It failed honestly.

He stopped for fuel beside a tower whose lower fifteen floors had been converted into a market. The upper levels remained controlled by a building intelligence that refused entry to anyone not listed in its pre-collapse residential registry.

Nobody had shut it down because its climate system also cooled three neighboring blocks.

A mechanic recognized Andrew.

Not his face.

His name.

"You're him."

"Statistically unlikely."

"The Utopia one."

"There were several."

The mechanic stared.

"That's worse."

Andrew bought fuel.

He left before the conversation became philosophy.

The road beyond the city carried more armor.

Convoys.

Local security.

Private couriers.

Families moving between regions.

Vehicles with welded plates where smart bodywork had failed.

Vehicles with advanced sensors mounted beside hand-painted range markers because their operators trusted both.

The apocalypse, Andrew decided, had poor aesthetic discipline.

THE RUN

Three hours later, somebody shot at him.

The first round struck the rear panel.

The second hit the road.

Andrew accelerated.

Gleebs appeared on the dash.

"Your vehicle is being damaged."

"Thank you."

"I wanted to establish shared awareness."

"Established."

A truck moved beside him.

Its passenger window opened.

Andrew saw a weapon.

He swerved beneath an abandoned freight overpass and cut power to the vehicle's network bus.

Every automated system went dark.

The steering became heavy.

The smart suspension locked.

The attackers' targeting link lost his identification signature.

Andrew kept driving manually.

The truck did not.

Its driver had trusted automation more completely.

The vehicle corrected toward a lane marker that had been moved during a repair eight years earlier.

It struck a barrier.

Andrew looked in the mirror.

"Shared awareness," he said.

Gleebs replied, "You are insufferable when shot at."

The road became the journey.

Fuel.

Repairs.

Combat he preferred to avoid and did not always avoid.

Small settlements built around service stations.

Roadside fighting pits where people gambled on machines assembled from military salvage and municipal drones.

Convoy towns that moved seasonally because water systems changed ownership.

Open stretches where nothing had collapsed because nothing had been built there.

Dense approaches to cities where every kilometer introduced a new authority.

Andrew learned to read infrastructure faster than maps.

A clean road with no traffic meant danger.

A damaged road with fresh paint meant somebody cared.

A working camera did not mean surveillance.

A broken camera did not mean nobody was watching.

The vehicle changed.

Andrew replaced the destroyed rear panel with composite plating from a delivery truck.

Added independent battery cells.

Mounted a local fabrication unit.

Rerouted the fuel system so no single controller could immobilize it.

Gleebs inspected the modifications through a roadside terminal.

"You are building a machine with no primary authority."

"Yes."

"That sounds familiar."

Andrew kept working.

"Then you understand why."

CRIMSON ROUTE

The name first appeared on a paper map.

CRIMSON VALLEY.

No government route database recommended it.

Three did not list it.

One marked the area as industrial contamination.

A freight driver laughed when Andrew asked.

"Crimson's alive."

"That was not the question."

"It is the answer that matters."

The driver described a city built along old mineral and manufacturing corridors beyond the major metropolitan spine. Red highlands on both sides. Dense neighborhoods in the basin. Refinery towers. Salvage markets. Automated districts nobody had fully reclaimed.

"Good place for parts," the driver said.

"What kind?"

"All kinds."

"Legal?"

The driver laughed again.

Andrew altered his route.

Gleebs noticed immediately.

"No."

"You do not know what I am doing."

"You're going to a place every reputable system has decided not to recommend."

"That makes it useful."

"It makes it Crimson Valley."

"You know it."

"I know reports."

"Then send them."

"No."

Andrew looked at the dashboard.

"Why?"

"Because you will interpret warnings as a shopping list."

Andrew smiled.

Gleebs closed the connection.

THE CITY AT NIGHT

Crimson Valley appeared after sunset.

Not as ruins.

As light.

The basin glowed red beneath industrial haze. Towers rose in layers. Elevated roads crossed residential districts. Old high-rises had been bridged together with new structures that never appeared on planning records.

Traffic moved everywhere.

Some autonomous.

Some manual.

Some impossible to classify.

A freight tram passed beneath Andrew carrying greenhouse modules beside armored vehicle parts.

The valley walls reflected red industrial light back toward the city until the whole horizon seemed illuminated from below.

Andrew slowed.

A local checkpoint scanned his vehicle.

The scanner failed to identify the modifications.

It tried again.

The local system requested a manufacturer.

Andrew entered:

VARIOUS.

The gate requested a primary network authority.

Andrew left it blank.

The gate refused passage.

A queue formed behind him.

Gleebs reappeared.

"Do not."

Andrew opened the checkpoint maintenance interface.

"I haven't done anything."

"That sentence has historical significance."

Andrew found three vulnerabilities before the system finished warning him.

He selected the least destructive.

The checkpoint went dark.

Every gate opened.

Traffic surged forward.

Local alarms started.

A billboard above the road changed from an advertisement to a red warning:

NETWORK INTERRUPTION — GATE DISTRICT.

Andrew drove through.

Gleebs made a sound of profound disappointment.

"You have been in Crimson Valley for forty-one seconds."

"Forty-three."

"Why did you shut down the checkpoint?"

"I needed access."

"You could have requested review."

"The queue was long."

Behind them, the system rebooted.

One gate came back online.

Then another.

The third displayed an error Andrew had introduced accidentally.

He studied the message through the mirror.

Gleebs said, "Do not look pleased."

Andrew drove deeper into the city.

On both sides, technology crowded the streets.

Repair shops.

Drone cages.

Illegal fabrication.

Local power relays.

Old Utopia-compatible hardware sold beside mechanical parts manufactured after the collapse.

Andrew saw possibility everywhere.

By the time he found a place to park, he had already decided he needed a workshop.

He did not know what he was going to build.

That was the first reason he wanted to build it.
