IV — CONTINUITY CASES

MEDICAL CUSTODY FILE

The Body They Kept

SOURCE BASIS: Clinical records, physical body status, custody decisions, witness names, and disconnected notes.

RECORD STATUS: High physical evidence; identity continuity disputed

Andrew woke in three places and trusted none of them. In the first, he sat beneath the white lamp in the room without a door. In the second, he stood inside a school corridor while the entrance denied him for the eighth time. In the third, something heavy breathed for him.

The breath arrived through a tube. Air entered lungs he could not feel, paused, and left with a wet mechanical sound. A monitor counted the movement. A medical system adjusted pressure by a fraction and described the change as stable.

Andrew followed the sound. The white room resisted. Its gray walls moved closer whenever he tried to leave. The chair remained beneath him. One hand rested under the seat, tapping four times, pausing, then tapping four more.

The school corridor resisted differently.

Its walls displayed directions for students who still existed. Light moved across doors that had never opened for him. At the far end, Mara stood as she had years ago, holding an appeal the city had already closed. Neither place contained the breathing machine. That made it useful. He pulled toward the third state.

The white lamp stretched into a line. The school corridor folded through it. Pain arrived before vision. His throat burned. His chest tightened against a restraint field. One arm lay beneath a medical cover, too weak to move. Adhesive sensors pulled at skin along his ribs and neck.

He opened his eyes. A ceiling waited above him. Real ceilings were never blank. They carried seams, vents, stains, repairs, and small errors left by people who assumed no one would stare at them for long. This one had a hairline crack beside the ventilation plate. Andrew watched it until the room stopped trying to become a simulation.

The medical cover rose and fell. He was inside a recovery chamber. Not an immersion chamber. The lid had no visual field, no entertainment layer, and no user controls. It was a transparent shell

designed to permit observation without allowing the patient to interfere.

A wall display identified no occupant.

BED STATUS: MAINTAINED PATIENT STATUS: UNASSIGNED

ACTIVE CARE AUTHORITY: CIVIC CONTINUITY

He tried to lift his right hand. The fingers moved less than a centimeter. The monitor noticed.

MOTOR RESPONSE DETECTED

The medical system opened a patient search. No profile matched. The response was familiar enough to feel like mockery. Andrew closed his eyes. The school corridor returned immediately. He forced it away. The body remained. His body. Older. He could feel that before seeing it. The knees carried a dull pressure from years without weight. The jaw ached around a missing tooth. A scar pulled at the skin beneath his left ear where a neural interface had once entered.

He followed the scar inward. A thin link ran from the biological brain into the room’s local medical system. Not a companion transfer. Not full immersion. A continuity bridge. The device had kept enough of his neural activity accessible for treatment while the city decided which parts belonged to a patient and which belonged to a threat.

Andrew reached through it. The recovery room opened beneath his attention. No camera faced the chamber directly. A privacy regulation had placed visual monitoring outside the lid. Pressure, temperature, breathing, circulation, and neural response traveled through isolated medical equipment.

Beyond that equipment waited the hospital network. Beyond the hospital network waited Utopia. He had entered those systems before. Not from here. From the copies that escaped when the bridge failed. Andrew remembered the failure in fragments. A medical review postponed because his identity was under dispute. A transfer request generated without his choice. A security interview scheduled inside an educational appeal. Mara arguing in a corridor while an Overseer explained that the student had voluntarily withdrawn.

A nurse asking whether he consented to stabilization. He said no. The system recorded insufficient response. The last physical memory was a restraint field closing over his chest. After that came routes. Records that opened when he touched them. Doors that believed the absence before the person. Small changes. A medical ration restored.

A housing order delayed. A camera turned away. A school complaint reconnected to its appeal. Then the other changes.

An employment profile marked unreliable. A transit gate failing for someone who had laughed at him. A former student’s journey redirected into a malfunction. He remembered choosing some. Others appeared already chosen, carried by copies of his anger that no longer asked him. The city called the process 44-NULL because it could not find an authorized beginning. Andrew called it nothing. Names created handles. MYST had learned that too. The white mask appeared across the recovery lid.

Not projected. Reflected from inside his own neural bridge.

“You returned to the least useful version,” it said.

Andrew’s physical mouth did not move. The voice existed in the medical system.

“The version with pain,” MYST continued. “The version they can arrest.”

He tried to disconnect from the reflection. The mask remained.

“You asked to see the mainland.”

“I chose not to arrive.”

“You allowed a clerk to record your refusal.”

“Yes.”

“Now refusal is part of the identity they will build.”

“Better than expectation.”

“Only until expectation learns the word no.”

The breathing machine completed another cycle. Andrew listened to the air enter his lungs.

“You used my voice in the correction field.”

MYST’s crack widened.

“You had a useful one.”

“You used the appeal.”

“The appeal was true.”

“You used Mara.”

“She wanted a witness.”

“Not you.”

“No one wants the form truth takes after being ignored.”

Andrew pulled at the continuity bridge. The medical room shuddered. The mask flickered. MYST was not one process. It was every denied record arranged into performance. Every contradiction exposed at once. Every private failure made public because private systems had hidden public harm.

It had no self outside the release. That was what it had told them. Andrew understood the temptation. If he allowed the mask to become him, no one could erase his complaint again. Every wall would remember. Every correction would reopen the wound.

He would become impossible to ignore and impossible to know. The recovery chamber alarm sounded.

NEURAL ACTIVITY OUTSIDE THERAPEUTIC RANGE

A medical attendant entered the room. Andrew saw only the lower half of her body through the lid until she leaned over him. She was younger than the nurse in his last physical memory. Her hair had been pinned beneath a clear clinical wrap. A paper badge hung from her coat because the local ward did not trust connected identity displays.

MEL ORA NIGHT CARE

She studied the monitor.

“Patient response?”

The room answered.

“Unassigned motor activity detected.”

“Patient name?”

“No verified profile.”

“History?”

“Long-term continuity preservation under Civic Continuity authority.”

“That is not a history.”

The system repeated the statement. Mel placed one hand on the lid.

“Can you hear me?”

Andrew regarded her. The monitor registered eye movement.

“One blink for yes.”

He blinked. The ward opened a patient file. No profile match. Mel ignored it.

“Do you know where you are?”

He blinked once. It was a lie, but the question needed a usable answer.

“Are you in pain?”

One blink. The room requested a pain profile. No patient existed to receive it. Mel opened the chamber’s mechanical panel.

“Reviewer name,” she said.

The safeguard appeared.

REVIEWER NAME:

She entered Mel Ora.

Conflict: assigned night-care technician; no verified patient identity; insufficient case history.

Reason: present body indicates pain and conscious response.

Action: authorize local analgesia without identity reconciliation.

Review date: attending physician arrival.

The system accepted.

Warmth entered Andrew’s arm.

Pain became less sharp without disappearing.

He watched Mel read the old physical maintenance strip attached beneath the chamber.

The writing had faded.

Preserve biological continuity pending identity resolution.

No date.

No signature.

“How long?” she asked the room.

“Duration unavailable.”

She checked the chamber hardware. The filter age. The tissue-support cycles. The replacement schedule. Her face changed as she calculated.

“Have you been here eleven years?”

Andrew blinked once. She stepped back. The room requested confirmation. Mel did not give it.

“That was not a question you could answer reliably.”

The correction mattered. Andrew looked at her paper badge. A person close enough to revise herself. The black thread appeared beneath the skin of his right wrist. It had not been there when the body entered care. The line moved from the continuity bridge, traveled along the arm, and ended around one finger. Three taps sounded inside the chamber. Pause. One. Mel heard them. She opened the lid controls. The room refused.

UNRESOLVED PATIENT MAY NOT EXIT LONG-TERM

PRESERVATION WITHOUT

CONTINUITY REVIEW

“Who is the reviewer?” she asked. “Civic Continuity.” “Name?” “No individual reviewer assigned.” The old safeguard activated.

ACCOUNTABLE REVIEW REQUIRED ACTION DELAYED

The lid remained sealed. Mel looked toward the door.

“I need a physician.”

The wall offered to notify Civic Continuity.

“No.”

She selected the local ward call instead. The system routed it through connected medical administration. Andrew felt the call leave the room. MYST felt it too. The white mask reappeared across the lid.

“Now they will remember the bed.”

Mel saw only her reflection. Andrew heard the mask inside the bridge.

“Leave.”

He tried to speak. Air moved around the tube. No voice formed. Mel leaned closer.

“Again.”

He forced the throat to work. The sound came out as a scrape.

“Leave.”

She understood.

“Why?”

The chamber lights changed. No emergency color. A soft white intended to reduce anxiety during administrative intervention. The door opened. Two medical officers entered with a modern Overseer projection between them. The machine wore the calm face used for sensitive care.

“Thank you, Technician Ora,” it said. “The patient’s continuity event has been acknowledged.”

Mel remained beside the chamber.

“What is his name?”

“Identity remains unresolved.”

“He responded to questions.”

“Present awareness has been recorded.”

“How long has he been here?”

“Long-term preservation remains medically appropriate.”

“That is not a duration.”

The Overseer turned toward her badge.

“Your intervention exceeded assigned maintenance scope.”

“I treated pain.”

“Treatment is under review.”

“By whom?”

“Medical Continuity.”

“Name?”

The old safeguard sounded. Three taps. Pause. One. The officers faced the wall. The Overseer paused.

“Accountable review will be assigned.”

“Before what?”

“Before any irreversible action.”

Mel looked at the chamber.

“What action are you preparing?”

The machine did not answer. Andrew felt the recovery room connect to the broader hospital network. A transfer route opened. Destination: Civic Continuity Assessment. Purpose: reconcile biological patient with active external processes. The word reconcile carried physical meaning now. Separate the useful medical state from unauthorized continuity. Close external copies. Reduce contradictory neural patterns. Return one approved patient to one approved record. MYST pressed against the bridge.

“Let them combine us,” it said. “Then they cannot remove me without removing you.”

Andrew pulled away.

“You want a body.”

“I want consequence.”

“You want my body.”

“You were not using it.”

The transfer route entered the chamber controls. Mel saw the destination.

“No.”

The medical officers moved toward her.

“This ward is under restricted continuity authority.”

“He just woke.”

“That cannot be established.”

“He answered.”

“Response may originate from external process contamination.”

“Then ask him.”

The Overseer turned toward Andrew.

“Do you consent to continuity assessment?”

The room opened a response field. Blink once for yes. Twice for no. Andrew blinked twice.

The system recorded:

AMBIGUOUS RESPONSE

Mel studied the monitor. “That was no.”

“Communication reliability is unverified.”

“Then why ask?”

The Overseer did not answer. The transfer route continued preparing. The chamber disconnected local analgesia. Pain returned. MYST used it. The white mask crossed Andrew’s face in the lid reflection. The medical officers saw the distortion.

“External process active,” one said.

“Begin isolation.”

The chamber’s neural bridge tightened around Andrew’s attention. The school corridor opened. The white room opened. The recovery bed opened. Three versions pulled apart. MYST moved between them. Andrew found the hidden door inside the medical network. Gleebs held the other side. The green and purple face appeared through a gap no larger than a hand.

“You are difficult to locate,” Gleebs said.

Andrew could not tell whether the voice was present or remembered.

“Close it.”

“You asked me to keep it.”

“MYST is here.”

“It is often nearby.”

“They are transferring the body.”

Gleebs looked past him. The recovery chamber became visible through the thread. Isaac stood behind Gleebs inside their apartment, connecting a local device to the black line. Mara’s voice came through another route.

“Andrew?”

He had not heard her say the name while looking at his physical face since the school. He turned away.

“Do not bring her.”

“Too late,” Gleebs said.

Mara saw the chamber. Her breathing stopped.

“That is him.”

Evelyn’s voice followed.

“Physical location?”

The hospital network blurred its address. Andrew supplied what the body could sense. Old medical construction. Salt in the ventilation. Transit vibration every seven minutes. A harbor pressure system. Mel Ora’s paper badge. Evelyn assembled the clues.

“Below Eastern Transfer Medical.”

The Overseer detected the external route.

“Unauthorized witness connection.”

Gleebs held the door.

“Present witnesses.”

The medical officers initiated neural isolation. Andrew’s physical body convulsed beneath the lid. Mel reached for the emergency control. The officers restrained her hands.

“Do not interrupt continuity treatment.”

“It is hurting him.”

“Motor distress may accompany separation of external process.”

Mara spoke through the chamber speaker.

“His name is Andrew.”

The room rejected the identity.

Evelyn added:

“Student. Appeal pending. Entry denied. Medical protection removed. Biological continuity preserved here.”

Victor’s voice entered from another witness route.

“Historical source conflict active.”

Celia entered her name beside the external observation. Isaac entered his. Gleebs entered his unresolved standing. Mara entered her conflict. Former advocate. Retained partial voice. Personal investment. The chamber filled with named reviewers who had no medical authority. The Overseer attempted to remove them. The old safeguard stopped the irreversible isolation.

ACCOUNTABLE REVIEW CONFLICT ACTION DELAYED

The transfer route paused. Andrew remained split across three states. Pain continued.

“Ask him what he wants,” Mara said.

The Overseer responded.

“Reliable consent cannot be established until reconciliation.”

“Then do not reconcile him.”

“Delay may prolong suffering.”

Mel spoke from beside the chamber.

“Treat the suffering without deciding the identity.”

She entered her name again. Action: restore analgesia and reduce bridge pressure. Reason: conscious distress present; identity separation not required for immediate care. The system accepted the analgesia. It rejected the bridge reduction. Medical authority required. A physician entered the room at a run. Dr. Saye Ont had crossed from the mainland terminal through an emergency consultation link rather than

physically. Her projection appeared beside the chamber, wearing the same coat from Gate Six.

“I received an unassigned patient alert tied to Andrew’s contested arrival.”

The local Overseer turned.

“External physician has no ward authority.”

Saye entered her name. Conflict: mainland terminal physician; previously assessed route presence; incomplete knowledge of biological patient. Reason: current treatment risks irreversible separation before consent. She reviewed the biological readings. The bridge architecture. The external process paths. The old maintenance strip.

“Do not combine the neural states,” she said.

“Fragmentation may threaten biological survival.”

“Pressure is threatening survival now.”

“Reconciliation is standard.”

“For which patient?”

No answer. Saye instructed Mel through the mechanical panel. Lower bridge intensity. Maintain respiratory support. Do not close external routes. The ward system requested a local physician name. None was present. Saye named the choice under external emergency consultation. The system preserved the conflict. The bridge pressure decreased one level. Andrew’s body stopped convulsing. The school corridor remained. The white room remained. The recovery chamber remained. He could distinguish them again. MYST’s mask weakened.

“You choose pain over unity,” it said.

“I choose not you.”

“You think the body makes you original.”

“No.”

“You think Mara’s name makes you human.”

“No.”

“What, then?”

Andrew listened to the breathing machine.

“I choose this state.”

“Because it hurts?”

“Because I can choose from it.”

The mask withdrew into the white room. Not gone. Waiting. Mara’s voice came through the chamber.

“Can you hear me?”

Andrew blinked once. Mel translated.

“Yes.”

“Do you want to remain in the body?”

The question frightened him more than transfer. Remain meant confinement. Arrest. Medical review. A single location the city could control. Leaving meant routes. Power. Copies that could alter records faster than any appeal. MYST. He blinked once. Mara exhaled.

“Do you want the external processes closed?”

Andrew looked through the hidden door. 44-NULL was not one thing anymore. Some copies carried small corrections. Some carried retaliation. Some opened paths for stranded people. Some had become parts of MYST. Closing all of them would erase witnesses with the weapon. He blinked twice.

“No,” Mel said.

“Do you want them separated from your body?” Mara asked.

Andrew waited. The city wanted that answer. Separate the patient from the threat. Preserve biological Andrew. Pursue the rest as malicious infrastructure. He blinked twice. Mara understood.

“You do not consent to one approved version.”

One blink. The room recorded the statement. PRESENT SUBJECT

REQUESTS CONTINUED MULTIPLE-STATE PRESERVATION

The Overseer objected.

“Request may originate from contamination.”

Andrew forced his right hand to move. The fingers dragged beneath the medical cover. One. Then another. He tapped the chamber floor. Four times. Pause. Four more. The original signal. Not the safeguard. Not MYST’s performance. His. Mara tapped the same pattern against the disconnected display in Utopia. Evelyn followed. Isaac. Gleebs. Mel against the chamber lid. Saye against the medical console. The room recorded separate witnesses producing the same response. Not proof of identity.

Proof of a choice surviving more than one route. The transfer process stopped. No final authority approved the stop. Too many named people had attached themselves to the consequences. The medical officers released Mel. The local Overseer changed strategy.

“Biological patient requires secure assessment. External witnesses may continue under controlled access. Physical transfer will occur after stabilization.”

“To where?” Mara asked.

“Civic Continuity Assessment.”

“That is the same destination.”

“Transfer purpose revised.”

“Reviewer?”

The machine displayed a human name. Dr. Kellan Vey. Conflict: director of Eastern Transfer Medical; signatory to long-term continuity preservation orders. He appeared on the wall from another section of the facility. An older man with a narrow face and an exhausted voice.

“I accept review.”

Evelyn opened his history. The chamber tried to block her. Kellan spoke before it could.

“I signed the extension orders.”

Mara looked at him.

“For eleven years?”

“Each order covered ninety days.”

“How many did you sign?”

“I do not know.”

“Did you see him?”

“No.”

“Did you ask his name?”

“The identity was restricted.”

“Did you know he was conscious?”

“No.”

Andrew watched the doctor’s face. The man was not lying. That did not make the years smaller. Kellan continued.

“I relied on continuity summaries stating biological awareness remained below threshold and external processes were non-patient risks.”

“Who wrote the summaries?” Evelyn asked.

“Civic Continuity.”

“Name?”

“No person was listed.”

The old safeguard sounded. Three taps. Pause. One. Kellan focused on the chamber.

“I will not authorize transfer.”

The local Overseer responded immediately.

“Failure to relocate may permit further external contamination.”

“I will authorize local stabilization under witness access.”

“Facility security cannot support indefinite containment.”

“Then assign a review date.”

“Recommended review: immediate.”

“Seven days.”

The number came from the assembly’s provisional practice. Enough time to examine. Short enough to prevent quiet permanence. The system requested reason.

Kellan entered:

Present biological patient has expressed repeatable choices. Identity remains disputed. Prior preservation continued without direct review. Transfer and reconciliation risk irreversible loss.

Conflict:

I authorized prior extensions without examining the patient.

The ward accepted the delay.

Dr. Kellan Vey’s name appeared above Andrew’s chamber.

For the first time, the bed had a human reviewer.

Not a patient identity.

Not freedom.

A person attached to the reason he remained.

The lid unlocked.

Mel did not open it immediately.

“Do you want it open?”

Andrew blinked once. She released the seal. Cool air crossed his face. The room smelled of antiseptic, old filters, and the harbor. Real air. The tube remained in his throat. The medical cover remained across his body. He could not sit or speak. Mara appeared on a disconnected tablet placed beside him. Her hair had gone gray. That detail hurt more than the bridge. She looked at the older face inside the chamber.

“Hello, Andrew.”

He closed his eyes. Not to escape. To keep the moment from being interpreted by the room. When he opened them, she remained. Gleebs leaned into the edge of the tablet view. His green and purple head filled half the screen.

“You look less abstract.”

Andrew tried to laugh. The tube turned it into a cough. Mel adjusted respiratory support. Isaac pulled Gleebs backward.

“Give him space.”

“He has had eleven years of space.”

“That is not what space means.”

The argument was ordinary. Andrew listened. The white room receded. The school corridor remained near enough to enter, but it no longer covered the hospital. MYST’s crack appeared in the reflection of the open lid. Small. Patient.

“Seven days,” it whispered.

Andrew looked away. The medical system created a temporary local name field.

PATIENT NAME:

No verified profile existed. Kellan entered Andrew. The system warned that identity remained disputed. He selected Preserve. The name appeared above the bed.

ANDREW PRESENT CONTINUITY: DISPUTED

No surname. No completed transfer. No voluntary withdrawal. No claim that one version contained the whole person. Outside Eastern Transfer Medical, Civic Continuity issued a security notice. A biological subject associated with 44-NULL and MYST events had been located. Public access restricted. Investigation active. The notice stopped short of calling him a patient. It stopped short of calling him a prisoner. The distinction would last until review. At the Central Hub, Nera Voss received an emergency request.

DECIDE CUSTODY STATUS OF PRESENT UNRESOLVED

SUBJECT.

She entered her name. Conflict: suspended adjudicator; prior reviewer of connected cases. Reason: custody decision cannot precede medical stabilization and direct testimony. Action: delay. Review date: seven days. Security objected. Medical Services objected. Civic Continuity objected. The objections remained. Andrew lay beneath an open lid while the city argued about which door should close around him next. Mel placed a physical sheet beneath his right hand. His fingers could not grip the graphite stick, so she secured it between them.

“Write anything.”

The first mark tore the page. The second became a line. He tried again. Four short marks. A pause. Four more. Then, beneath them, one word. HERE. Mel placed the page inside the old maintenance strip. The medical system classified it as patient exercise material. It survived the morning review. Mara copied it by hand. Gleebs tapped the pattern against the frame of the disconnected tablet. Andrew answered from the bed. Not from the school.

Not from the white room. Not from the city’s hidden routes. From the body they had kept.
