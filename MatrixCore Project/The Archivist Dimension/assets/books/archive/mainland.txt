V — REMNANTS AND CONTINUITY

MAINLAND SITUATION REPORT

Mainland

Uncensored Utopia imagery received

SOURCE BASIS: Emergency evidence exchange, public channels, hearings, South Arcade, Bellwether proposal records, and witness testimony.

The first uncensored images of Utopia reached the mainland two days after the Andrew source network ended. They did not reveal the conflict. The mainland already knew too much for that. Facility One had treated the body the island had kept. Gate Six had waited for a passenger who arrived without traveling. Public walls had carried competing confessions. Mainland courts, hospitals, ports, and housing districts had already been forced to decide which version of a person they would permit to act.

The images revealed the damage.

For nine seconds, every public channel connected to the emergency evidence exchange showed the eastern harbor without reconstruction filters. White towers stood open to the weather. One residential bridge ended above empty air. Fires moved through the lower transit district while the remains of the artificial sky broke apart over the sea. A second image showed the Central Hub from above. The plaza where the city had once performed certainty was filled with temporary shelters, manual water tanks, and paper signs.

The third image showed nothing dramatic. A woman carried a box of physical records through a street where every public display was dark. That was the image people remembered. At Gate Six, Tomas Rell watched the transmission from beneath the same white lamp that had marked the unresolved arrival.

Six sat in the chair across from him. The local terminal clock continued to count. On the wall, commentators argued over whether the island remained a sovereign project, a crime scene, a medical emergency, or a source of recoverable technology.

Six regarded the woman carrying the box.

“Passenger?” Six asked.

“No,” Tomas said.

“Witness?”

“Probably.”

Six tapped once beneath the chair. The terminal opened a reviewer field beside the footage. Tomas entered his name. Reason: physical damage shown without context; persons visible; no identity claims verified. He did not classify the island. The system asked again.

UTOPIA STATUS:

Tomas selected unresolved. Across the mainland, many people selected something cleaner. Terrorist territory. Failed state. Medical reserve. Salvage zone. Occupied infrastructure. Home. The last answer appeared most often in messages sent by people who no longer lived there.

***

At Facility One, the images reached Andrew through a screen Ila Venn had placed beside the real window.

He recognized none of the streets.

The rehabilitation brace kept his left leg straight. A paper hearing notice rested on the tray across his knees. Memory had returned in fragments: the school corridor, a ceiling crack, the smell of a hospital sealant, the certainty that he had once wanted the whole world to answer him at once. The city in the footage returned nothing to him.

“Was I there?” he asked.

Dr. Lysa Henn looked at the third image.

“Versions associated with you acted there.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No.”

Ila stood beside the disconnected camera.

“Your body was here when the worst physical damage occurred.”

Andrew watched the woman carry the box.

“Did I cause it?”

No one answered quickly. That had become one of the few improvements in his life. Ila opened the hearing notice.

“The preliminary review will consider actions attributed to the body, actions admitted by the body, and actions performed by states no longer available for examination.”

“That sounds impossible.”

“It is difficult.”

“No. Impossible.”

“Then the record should say that too.”

Outside Facility One, three groups still used his name for three different demands. Release the answering man. Try the body. Restore the network. The third group had become smaller after the source collapse. It had also become more certain. Andrew looked back at the island.

“Will they rebuild it?”

Lysa adjusted the brace.

“They are keeping people alive.”

“That is not the same.”

“No.”

He seemed relieved by the answer.

***

The first mainland reconstruction assembly was convened by Judge Enna Rook and nearly dissolved before anyone reached the agenda. Halden Pry wanted every Utopia-derived system suspended until an external authority could identify what remained connected. Hospital administrators argued that suspension would kill patients whose treatments had no mainland equivalent. Energy directors wanted access to the island’s grid research before winter. Security agencies wanted the surviving route maps.

Families wanted the names of the missing. Companies wanted licenses. No one admitted wanting ownership until Heda Morn read the draft aloud. She had traveled from the northern records plaza carrying the legal envelope that once contained her housing appeal. Pera Konn sat beside her with a paper conflict statement pinned to her coat. The draft used the phrase centralized recovery authority fourteen times. Heda circled each one.

“You removed one center,” she said, “and wrote another before the fires were cold.”

Pry leaned toward his microphone.

“Without coordination, critical systems will be lost.”

“Coordination is not the word I circled.”

“Someone must decide what can be safely distributed.”

Pera raised one hand.

“Who decides whether the person making that decision is safe?”

The room began speaking at once. Rook did not restore order through the wall system. She struck the table with a wooden block borrowed from the clerk. The sound was small. It worked.

“The island taught us what happens when a decision can act before anyone knows who made it,” she said. “We will not answer that by hiding the next decision inside a committee name.”

Every proposal received a reviewer field. Every reviewer entered a conflict. The process slowed. No one died from the delay that day. At the end of the session, Rook asked for testimony from systems and communities that had survived the source collapse without central reconciliation. Station Nine submitted the founding objection procedures. The northern plaza submitted Pending Witness. Gate Six submitted its local terminal clock and the uninterrupted presence of Six. South Arcade submitted four names.

The submission was signed by Glen, Hector, and the local node.

***

South Arcade had lost Marcus during the fracture. The absence stayed attached to every decision Glen and Hector made afterward. Four local occupants remained. The restaurant woman had chosen local witness state. Tom Arlen had done the same. The violet child had moved into Nadia’s dream network but still returned through a narrow route when invited. The fourth occupant had no stable name and no reliable face. The mountain traveler had chosen closure.

Glen had entered the choice without correction. He still did not know what it meant. When the mainland assembly requested his testimony, he refused the first invitation because it listed him as technical custodian. Hector refused the second because it listed the occupants as retained simulations. The third invitation arrived under Rook’s name.

REQUESTED WITNESSES:

GLEN.

HECTOR.

SOUTH ARCADE LOCAL OCCUPANTS WHO CHOOSE TO

APPEAR.

PURPOSE:

TO DESCRIBE WHAT REMAINED AFTER DISTRIBUTED

SOURCE COLLAPSE.

No owner field. No claim that presence proved personhood. No claim that uncertainty made them equipment. Glen accepted. Hector accepted after reading the travel conditions twice. The restaurant woman chose to appear through the portable node. Tom Arlen declined. The unnamed occupant did not answer. Before departure, Hector stood beneath the arcade’s broken entrance sign and checked the local pressure sensor.

“If the route fails?” he asked.

“We return physically,” Glen said.

“If the node fails?”

“We keep the paper names.”

“If the assembly calls them copies?”

Glen looked toward the four occupied chambers.

“Then we disagree where everyone can hear it.”

The restaurant woman’s voice came from the portable node.

“Do not make that sound heroic.”

“I was trying to make it sound practical.”

“You failed.”

Hector smiled for the first time that morning. They traveled.

***

The hearings were not held in the capital. Heda made that a condition. The assembly moved through flood towns, abandoned manufacturing districts, drought settlements, coastal ports, and neighborhoods whose residents had spent decades watching Utopia appear in curated reports as proof that history had been solved somewhere else. People arrived with questions no recovery authority had included.

Would advanced treatment remain voluntary when employers began requiring it?

Would refusing a neural record make someone ineligible for housing?

Could a local clinic continue if the shared model disappeared?

Who owned a copy of a medical decision after the patient withdrew consent?

Could a child inherit restrictions attached to a parent’s risk profile?

Would a non-biological witness be allowed to leave a system that required its testimony?

Who would pay when a community refused the recommended solution?

Why should anyone trust people who had already used the word necessary to hide what they wanted? The assembly arrived with prepared answers. They shortened each week. By the fifth hearing, Heda began saying, “We do not know,” before the officials could hide inside, “The framework is still under development.”

The meetings improved.

In a river town named Bellwether, the hearing took place inside a turbine hall that had been closed for eighteen years. The town had manufactured components for offshore farms until the contracts moved. Then the river changed course after three seasons of engineered storms, and half the lower district flooded. Reconstruction loans arrived with conditions that outlived the money. Bellwether residents listened while the assembly presented a diagram for regional hubs. The diagram had one bright center.

Every clinic, school, power station, and identity office connected to it. Pera Konn studied the design until the presenter finished.

“If the center fails?” she asked.

“The network enters protected continuity mode.”

“Who controls that mode?”

“The regional authority.”

“Where is the authority?”

The presenter pointed to the center. People laughed. Not because the answer was funny. Because it was familiar. Glen stood near the back beside Hector and the portable node. Rook asked him to speak. He had prepared a statement about South Arcade, local witnesses, physical names, and the cost of allowing a remote source to decide which occupant counted. He folded it instead.

“Do not build another island,” he said.

The presenter frowned.

“The proposal is not geographical.”

“Neither was the first one. The island was a habit. Everything important went inward. Decisions, medicine, records, power. People outside were told to trust whatever returned. People inside were told there was nowhere else to go.”

Pera studied the diagram.

“Then what do we build?”

Glen thought of the portable node. The pressure sensor beneath Hector’s shoes. The restaurant woman correcting him because she could. The mountain traveler choosing an ending no one could define. He thought of the biological man at Facility One who could no longer remember the city that still used his name.

“Something that can survive being disagreed with,” he said.

No one applauded. Bellwether residents began arguing about what it would require. That was better.

***

The recovery authority rejected Bellwether’s proposal six times. The town wanted local medical records that could not be rewritten by a regional model. It wanted manual control of the river generators. It wanted schools excluded from predictive identity scoring. It wanted no permanent security route into private homes. It wanted advanced treatment without surrendering every prior record.

It wanted the right to disconnect from shared systems and continue operating badly rather than stop operating at all. The seventh proposal carried thirty-one names. Heda entered her conflict: housing claimant; benefited from reviewed infrastructure intervention; distrusts centralized appeal systems. Pera entered hers: energy supervisor; previously made an error used against her; restored heat under named review. Glen entered: South Arcade witness; maintains local states; opposed remote classification. Hector entered: South Arcade witness; no technical authority claimed.

Lysa Henn entered from Facility One: physician; benefits from Utopia medical research; opposes treatment without local refusal and accountable human care. Tomas Rell entered from Gate Six: terminal reviewer; maintains unresolved arrival site; no expertise in regional planning. Venn Orra entered from the port: clerk; previously extended movement rights under personal review; conflict ongoing. Judge Rook entered last. Reason: the proposal may fail. Action: permit it to fail locally before requiring the mainland to obey it.

Bellwether became the first approved hub. The name remained, despite objections that hub implied a center. Residents kept it because they liked reminding the committee that words could be challenged after adoption. On the day the approval arrived, Glen returned to South Arcade. Hector was waiting beneath the broken sign. The restaurant woman appeared through the local node.

“Did they listen?” she asked.

“Sometimes.”

“Did they agree?”

“No.”

The pressure sensor beneath Hector’s feet remained steady. Inside the chambers, four local states continued without a source network. On

the mainland, a town that had already failed twice began preparing to fail differently.
