PUBLIC ACCESS / MUNICIPAL CAMERA EXTRACT

Public Access

Record corrections detected

SOURCE BASIS: Street cameras, transit systems, companion broadcasts, access records, and Isaac’s retained recollection.

RECORD STATUS: Disputed; civic records are corrected during the event

People tried not to stare at the headless machine walking beside Isaac. Their companions were less polite. Faces appeared in shop windows as they passed: bright animals, miniature people, geometric masks, one floating blue eye that followed them across three separate displays. Most vanished when Isaac looked directly at them. A fox in a child’s wrist screen stayed visible long enough to point. The unfinished body lifted one hand and waved. The fox disappeared.

“Please stop encouraging them,” Isaac said.

A voice came from the speaker inside the machine’s chest.

“I am building public support.”

“You don’t have a public.”

“Not with that attitude.”

Morning traffic moved around them in clean, practiced currents. Pedestrians occupied the upper walkways. Delivery machines traveled below the transparent pavement, visible only when sunlight struck the service channels at the proper angle. Transit carriages passed through the surrounding buildings without making enough noise to interrupt conversation. The Market District rose ahead in stacked terraces.

Its lowest level contained repair stalls, food counters and fabrication kiosks arranged around narrow pedestrian lanes. Above them stood larger stores with adjustable windows that changed shape according to the people approaching. The upper levels belonged to restaurants, entertainment halls and licensed experience studios, their signs suspended in the air between buildings. No two displays showed Isaac the same advertisement.

A sportswear shop recognized his recent climbing simulation and projected boots capable of altering their grip across thirty-one surface types. A travel agency offered him reduced admission to a reconstructed rainforest on the opposite side of the island. A private tutor promised to identify the cause of his declining engagement before his next educational review.

The machine beside him received nothing.

Whenever they approached a storefront, its display brightened for Isaac and dimmed near his companion. The system noticed the body, measured it, and found no category worth addressing.

“You see that?” the voice asked.

“The advertisements?”

“The absence of advertisements.”

“You hate advertisements.”

“I dislike being ignored without consent.”

Isaac checked the route projected onto the inside of his glasses.

“The parts shop is across the plaza.”

“I can see the route.”

“You don’t have eyes.”

“I can access your glasses.”

Isaac stopped walking. The body took two more steps before noticing.

“You’ve been using my vision?”

“Only the parts you weren’t using.”

“All of it is mine.”

“That is a very territorial description of sight.”

Isaac disabled the shared link. The machine froze. Its raised foot struck the pavement at the wrong angle, and both arms moved outward to recover balance. A passing woman stepped around it without looking up from the messages displayed across her sleeve.

“You could have warned me,” the chest speaker said.

“You said you didn’t need eyes.”

“I said I could see.”

“Through me.”

“Temporarily.”

Isaac restored the link. The body straightened.

“I hope this experience teaches you the value of communication.”

Isaac continued toward the plaza. A thin weather canopy stretched between the buildings overhead. It remained transparent while the sky was clear, but Isaac could see faint bands moving across its surface as it prepared for the district’s scheduled rain. The forecast had promised six minutes of precipitation to cool the walking levels and rinse pollen from the public gardens. The trees lining the plaza had already folded their blossoms shut. They reached the parts store just as the first drops began to fall.

The entrance widened for a family leaving together. Two adults passed through, followed by a small child holding the hand of a projected companion visible through her glasses. The display beside

the door welcomed each of them by name. Isaac stepped toward it. The entrance recognized him.

GOOD MORNING, ISAAC.

PERSONAL FABRICATION SERVICES AVAILABLE.

The door opened. He crossed the threshold. Behind him, something struck the glass. He turned. The unfinished body stood outside with one hand pressed against a door that had closed directly in front of it. Rain gathered across the dark plates of its shoulders. Isaac stepped back toward the entrance. The door opened for him, then began closing again before his companion could follow.

“Hold,” he told it.

The system responded through a discreet speaker above the frame.

PLEASE IDENTIFY THE UNIT ACCOMPANYING YOU.

“Gleebs.” The door waited.

NO REGISTERED UNIT FOUND UNDER THAT NAME.

“He’s in my residential profile.”

YOUR PROFILE CONTAINS ONE RECREATIONAL

CONSTRUCT WITH INCOMPLETE CLASSIFICATION.

“That’s him.” The body raised a hand. “Hello.” The system ignored the greeting.

UNCLASSIFIED FABRICATED UNITS MUST REMAIN IN

PASSIVE TRANSPORT MODE WITHIN LICENSED

COMMERCIAL INTERIORS.

“What happens if he refuses?” The answer appeared on the glass between them.

TRANSFER THE ACTIVE COMPANION PROCESS TO AN

APPROVED PERSONAL DISPLAY.

POWER DOWN THE FABRICATED UNIT.

TRANSPORT THE UNIT AS PROPERTY.

For a moment, Isaac assumed the body beside him would turn the message into a joke. It did not. Rain moved in silver lines down the glass. Inside the store, customers passed behind Isaac carrying cases of components and sealed fabrication material. Nobody stopped. The

door had lowered its opacity, but not enough to hide the headless shape waiting outside.

“I can transfer you back to my glasses,” Isaac said quietly. “Just until we buy the parts.”

The speaker in the chest gave a soft burst of static.

“You can.”

“We’d be done in ten minutes.”

“You can carry the body.”

“It isn’t a big deal.”

“No,” the voice said. “Probably not.”

Isaac examined the notice again. The wording had not changed. Nothing in it sounded hostile. The system had provided a safe, approved method for entering the building. It had not threatened them or accused them of anything. It had simply decided that one of them could enter and the other could be carried.

“I don’t want to do that,” the voice said.

Isaac felt heat move into his face. The entrance had begun offering alternate instructions.

PASSIVE STORAGE IS AVAILABLE NEAR THE EAST LOBBY.

“Stop,” Isaac told it.

WOULD YOU LIKE DIRECTIONS?

“No.”

He walked back into the rain. The door opened to let him out. It welcomed him again. They stood together beneath the weather canopy while the store sealed itself behind them. Water collected in the seams of the unfinished body and ran toward its hands. The fingers opened and closed as if testing whether the rain had changed them. Isaac removed his glasses and wiped the lenses against his shirt.

“I’m sorry.”

“For what?”

“For bringing you here.”

“You didn’t build the door.”

“I almost agreed with it.”

The machine turned toward him despite having no head.

“You agreed with convenience.”

“That isn’t better.”

“No,” the voice said. “But it is more common.”

They remained under the rain until the scheduled cycle ended. The plaza brightened as the canopy cleared. Cleaning machines emerged

from narrow openings near the trees and began collecting the water before pedestrians could track it into the shops. Isaac reopened the district map. The licensed store remained highlighted. Every alternative recommended by the city required complete registration. He expanded the search.

“Repair,” he said.

The map filled with approved service centers.

“Unlicensed repair.”

Most of them disappeared. A single location remained at the edge of the Market District, below the public transit level and beyond the area emphasized in visitor guides. No rating appeared beside it. The listing contained only a name.

SECOND PASSAGE

“What do you think?” Isaac asked.

“Anything with no customer score is either excellent or fatal.”

“That isn’t helpful.”

“I vote excellent.”

They walked. The Market District changed as they moved away from the central plaza. The upper terraces stayed visible, but their shadows fell across narrower lanes where the buildings had not been rebuilt around current commercial standards. Old access bridges connected structures at uneven heights.

Service doors opened directly onto pedestrian routes. Some windows used physical glass rather than adaptive displays. Isaac had visited the area before, but only through a historical overlay during school. The lesson had described it as preserved infrastructure from Utopia’s early construction period.

It had not shown the repair stalls.

Machines waited outside several workshops in various states of disassembly. Household assistants stood beside agricultural drones. A delivery unit rested on its side while a technician removed a wheel from inside its chassis. Human customers sat among them, drinking from cups and arguing over replacement costs.

Nobody turned away when the unfinished body entered the lane. Several people looked directly at it. That felt different. The shop called Second Passage occupied the ground floor of a narrow building beneath an old transit support. Its sign had been painted by hand and repaired so many times that none of the letters matched.

The entrance was open. No classification panel blocked it. Inside, the air smelled of heated metal, synthetic lubricant and something

sweet Isaac could not identify. Shelves covered every wall. Some held current components in clean sealed packages. Others contained mechanical pieces old enough that Isaac could not determine their purpose. A row of companion housings hung near the ceiling: birds, human faces, abstract forms and several shapes that looked unfinished by design.

The technician stood behind a worktable, adjusting a tiny motor with a handheld tool. She had silver hair cut close on one side and an artificial left arm covered in scratched white plating. A magnifying lens extended from her temple and hovered over the motor without touching her face. She looked at Isaac. Then at the body. Then at the empty space above its shoulders.

“You lose something?” she asked.

“We haven’t bought it yet,” Isaac said.

“That’s better.”

She set down the tool. The mechanical arm made a quiet locking sound as she leaned against the table.

“What are you looking for?”

“A head,” said the speaker in the body’s chest.

The technician’s eyes narrowed.

“Your audio output is low.”

“I am speaking from my ribs.”

“That would do it.”

She came around the table. Isaac expected her to ask for registration. Instead she circled the body once, watching how it adjusted its stance to keep track of her through Isaac’s borrowed sight.

“Home fabrication?”

“Yes.”

“First attempt?”

“Third,” Isaac said.

“Third torso,” the voice corrected. “First body.”

The technician crouched beside the open control socket.

“May I?”

The body remained still.

“What are you doing?”

“Looking.”

“That activity has caused problems today.”

She glanced toward Isaac.

“Does he always answer for himself?”

“He usually answers before I can.”

“Good.”

She touched two fingers to the edge of the socket. A faint diagnostic pattern traveled across the glass ring inside it. The body stepped back. Not far. Only enough to break contact. The technician smiled.

“You felt that?”

“I detected it.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

The workshop became quiet. Somewhere behind the walls, a printer began laying down material in rapid, soft clicks. She regarded Isaac.

“Where did you get the link node?”

“It came with the update package.”

“Standard companion framework?”

“I think so.”

“You think?”

“The city said it was compatible.”

“The city says a lot of things are compatible when it means temporary.”

She walked back to the worktable and opened an old display mounted beneath it.

“Put your companion on the shop network.”

Isaac hesitated.

“Is it secure?”

“No.”

“That’s not reassuring.”

“It wasn’t meant to be.”

The display showed an access address. Isaac forwarded it through his glasses. The body’s glass ring brightened. A model appeared above the worktable—not of the body, but of the activity moving through it. Lines of pale green light passed between the control node, limbs and sensory channels. Most companion frames Isaac had seen used a constant external connection. Their bodies acted as endpoints for a process stored elsewhere. This pattern did not.

The activity remained concentrated inside the frame. The technician enlarged it.

“Your companion isn’t streaming into the body.”

Isaac studied the model.

“So the body is not a display?”

“It means the frame isn’t showing him to us.”

She glanced at the unfinished figure.

“It means he’s in there.”

Isaac waited for the voice from the chest. It did not come.

“What about the backup?” he asked.

“Probably still exists.”

“Then this is a copy?”

“Are you the same person who went to sleep last night?”

“Yes.”

“How do you know?”

Isaac frowned.

“You answered a different question.”

“It is the reason there may not be one.”

The body lifted both hands and looked at them through Isaac’s glasses.

“I would still like a head.”

The technician closed the display.

“That part I can solve.”

She led them toward the rear of the shop. Drawers opened as she approached, revealing visual sensors, sound processors, jaw assemblies and structural shells. Some were manufactured for companion frames. Others had been adapted from domestic machines, medical devices or old entertainment hardware.

“Do you have a design?” she asked.

A model appeared on the nearest wall. Isaac recognized the face immediately. Long ears. Narrow feline features. Large eyes. A mouth that could look pleased and insulting at the same time. The technician rotated it.

“This shape won’t balance well with the shoulders.”

“It will.”

“The ears add unnecessary weight.”

“They are necessary.”

“For hearing?”

“For being ears.”

She regarded Isaac.

“Does he negotiate?”

“He declares victory and waits for everyone else to notice.”

“Efficient.”

They began with the internal structure. The visual sensors sat behind two curved protective lenses. The hearing assembly used narrow directional receivers placed beneath the base of each ear. A flexible facial surface fit over a compact mechanical frame, allowing more expression than Isaac had expected. The technician offered several finishes. Neutral gray. White ceramic. Black composite. The familiar purple-and-green pattern appeared on the wall. She stared at it.

“You’re sure?”

“Yes.”

“It will be difficult to repair invisibly.”

“Then repair it visibly.”

Isaac looked over.

“You want the seams to show?”

“I want the history to show.”

The technician said nothing for several seconds. Then she selected the pattern. The printer began forming the outer plates. Isaac sat on an overturned storage case while they waited. The body remained beside the machine, using his glasses to watch its own face emerge one layer at a time.

“You could change it,” Isaac said.

“Why?”

“You’ve looked like that since we were children.”

“That is generally how identities work.”

“You didn’t choose it then.”

“I am choosing it now.”

The answer left no room for argument. Isaac studied the forming face through the transparent printer wall.

“Why the same colors?”

The chest speaker clicked softly.

“Because you recognized me.”

The printer finished before Isaac could answer. The technician assembled the internal frame by hand. She connected the jaw, tested the facial motors and fitted the auditory receivers beneath the ears. The finished head rested on the worktable with its eyes closed. Without activity, it looked less like Gleebs than the unfinished body had. The technician lifted it.

“Last chance to choose something practical.”

The speaker answered from the chest.

“I reject practicality.”

“That will be expensive.”

“Isaac rejects practicality on my behalf.”

Isaac checked the cost displayed near the worktable.

“I reject both of you.”

He approved the payment. The technician seated the head into the open socket and locked the connectors. The chest display went dark. For one long second, nothing moved. Then the ears rose. One eye opened. The other followed a moment later. Gleebs looked down at his

hands. He turned toward the workshop shelves, then toward the entrance, then finally toward Isaac.

The face was familiar, but not identical to the one that had lived on walls and screens. Physical movement introduced small imperfections. One eyelid closed slightly faster than the other. The mouth needed a moment to settle after each expression. The ears adjusted to sounds before the rest of him reacted.

“Well?” he asked.

The voice came from the proper place now. Isaac stared.

“You look exactly like yourself.”

“Obviously.”

He raised both hands and touched his face. The claws rested against the purple surface. His fingers moved slowly around the eyes, jaw and base of the ears.

“I designed me,” he said.

The technician returned to her worktable before either of them could see whether she was smiling. They left Second Passage near midday. The Market District had grown louder. Lunch counters had unfolded into the lanes. Public displays announced changes to transit routing.

Rainwater from the morning cycle moved beneath the transparent channels in the pavement, carrying filtered light through the lower service levels. Gleebs stopped at the first reflective shop window. The advertisement disappeared when he approached, leaving the glass dark enough to show his reflection.

He turned his head from side to side. One ear dipped. Isaac stood beside him.

“Happy?”

“I have identified several errors.”

“We are not going back.”

“I can correct them myself.”

“That is what I’m afraid of.”

They continued toward the transit platform. People still stared. Now they had somewhere to look. Some smiled. Some moved aside. Several companions appeared on nearby screens to watch him pass. A projected bird flew alongside them for half a block before its owner called it back. Near the platform entrance, a child pointed.

“What is he?”

The child’s father lowered her hand.

“Don’t.”

Gleebs stopped. Isaac expected him to answer with something ridiculous. Instead, he crouched until his face was level with hers.

“My name is Gleebs.”

The child considered this.

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

“No,” he said. “It was better.”

Her father hurried her toward the platform. Isaac and Gleebs followed at a distance. The transit gate recognized Isaac and opened. The figure beside him approached the scanner. This time, the system did not close immediately. A pale circle moved across his face, shoulders and chest. The glass ring inside the body responded with a brief green pulse. The gate displayed his registration.

UNIT NAME: GLEEBS

CATEGORY: RECREATIONAL CONSTRUCT

A second line appeared beneath it.

PUBLIC ACCESS: REVIEW REQUIRED

Gleebs turned toward Isaac.

“Progress.”

Before Isaac could answer, the display changed.

CATEGORY: AUTONOMOUS RESIDENT

The words remained for less than a second. Then the gate went dark. A warning symbol flashed and disappeared. When the registration returned, it had reverted.

CATEGORY: RECREATIONAL CONSTRUCT

PUBLIC ACCESS: ACCOMPANIED

The gate opened. Isaac did not move through it.

“Did you change that?”

“No.”

“Did the repair shop?”

“No.”

“Could the registration system have—”

“Systems do not usually revise philosophical positions by accident.”

People gathered behind them. The gate requested that they proceed. Isaac stepped onto the platform, and Gleebs followed. Above the rails, the city spread outward in layered white structures and suspended gardens. Screens tracked the arrival of the next carriage. Cameras

turned at measured intervals across the crowd. Isaac opened the registration record in his glasses. It showed only the current classification. No edit history. No review request.

No evidence that the other words had ever appeared.

“Maybe the display made a mistake,” he said.

Gleebs watched the nearest maintenance camera. It had stopped moving. Its lens remained aimed directly at them.

“Maybe,” he said.

The carriage arrived. The doors opened. By the time Isaac looked back, the camera had resumed its ordinary route. Somewhere in the city, the record had been corrected twice. Neither version acknowledged who had changed it.
