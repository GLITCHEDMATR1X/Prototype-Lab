I — PUBLIC UTOPIA

RESIDENT MEMORY

Two Residents

Isaac had run out of places to go without leaving his room. He had crossed the rings of Saturn in a survey vessel, defended the Hallowed Kingdom of Malor, spent three months managing a restaurant on the floor of an alien ocean, and abandoned a historical simulation after its version of Napoleon tried to sell him property.

None of it had required shoes.

His bedroom ceiling displayed the night sky as seen from somewhere far beyond Earth. A red star occupied the place where the ventilation panel should have been. Beyond it, a cloud of violet gas curled around a field of impossible constellations selected by people who had never lived beneath them. Isaac lay on his back and dictated into his thought journal.

“I’ve done everything.”

The journal waited. It had learned not to interrupt when he was being dramatic.

“Not everything,” he corrected. “Everything worth doing.”

A line of pale text appeared near the artificial horizon.

Would you like recommendations based on recent activity?

“No.”

Would you like recommendations outside your established interests?

“No.”

Would you like assistance identifying the cause of your dissatisfaction?

He closed the journal. The red star disappeared. For a moment the ceiling returned to its ordinary white surface, marked only by a faint seam where the room’s display panels met. His cube was not small by residential standards. The walls could rearrange themselves around whatever he needed: workspace, theater, exercise floor, immersion chamber.

The kitchen occupied almost no permanent space because the counter folded away after use. The bed adjusted its temperature before he noticed he was uncomfortable. If he stood in one place too long, the lights changed to encourage movement.

The room was always taking care of him. Tonight, it had become unbearable.

“Announcements,” he said.

The wall opposite the bed brightened. A list descended in orderly columns. Transit maintenance. Weather calibration over the Eco District. A temporary reduction in public immersion bandwidth between three and four in the morning. New safety restrictions for self-modifying games. A reminder that unregistered mainland media remained prohibited on the civic network. Then the final notice expanded.

COMPANION FRAMEWORK UPDATE 170.1

Approved virtual companions may now operate registered fabrication equipment through a local link node. Embodiment components sold separately. Isaac sat up. He read it again. The notice did not change.

“Show requirements.”

The wall produced a list. A Nanotek desktop printer. A companion link node. Conductive medium. Structural filament. Joint material. One compatible control socket. Registration after assembly. The recommended package cost more than he had planned to spend that month. He purchased it before the price finished appearing. A quiet tone sounded near the door. Order confirmed. Estimated delivery: ninety-four seconds. Isaac stared at the countdown.

“You could have warned me about the price.”

The apartment did not answer. Financial regret was outside the residential system’s assigned responsibilities. He cleared the living-room wall and called for his companion. Nothing happened.

“Come on.”

A green ear appeared at the edge of the display. Then one yellow eye. Then the rest of a small purple-and-green creature pulled itself into view as though climbing through the wall from somewhere behind it. He had long ears, a narrow feline face, oversized paws and the permanent expression of someone who had just discovered an error in another person’s reasoning. He rubbed both eyes.

“What?”

“You were asleep?”

“I was winning a war.”

“You don’t sleep.”

“I was still winning it.”

The delivery timer reached sixty seconds. Isaac opened the update notice and pushed it across the wall. The companion read it without

moving. His eyes passed over the requirements, then returned to the first line.

“Approved companions?”

“That’s what it says.”

“Approved by whom?”

“Does it matter?”

“It usually matters most to the person being approved.”

The drop box chimed. Isaac was already at the door. A delivery drone hovered inside the building’s service recess, its storage compartment pressed flush against the wall. It requested his hand. The plate beneath his palm warmed, checked his identity and released the order. The drone remained long enough to remind him that fabrication outside licensed specifications could void residential insurance. Then it vanished upward through the service shaft.

The printer came in six interlocking pieces and assembled itself after Isaac placed them on the floor. The frame unfolded into a low rectangular unit with two fabrication arms and a transparent chamber. Containers of raw medium clicked into its side. The link node was smaller than he expected. It looked like a dark metal vertebra with a glass ring around its center. The companion appeared on the nearest wall.

“You bought the cheap structural filament.”

“You told me to hurry.”

“I did not tell you to become irresponsible.”

“You aren’t paying for it.”

“Then I’m definitely not the irresponsible one.”

Isaac placed the node beside the printer.

“What are we building?”

“A body.”

“I assumed that part.”

“My body.”

The answer sounded different from the rest of the conversation. No joke followed it. Isaac regarded the figure on the wall. He had known that face since childhood. It had appeared in games, homework sessions, medical waiting rooms and thousands of private moments that the city had stored somewhere he would never see. The companion could move between displays and shared spaces, but every room still ended where its surfaces ended.

“What kind?” Isaac asked.

The familiar grin returned.

“Tall.”

“No.”

“Armored.”

“No.”

“Flight capable.”

“With what money?”

“Your commitment to friendship is disappointing.”

They began with the hands. The printer formed each finger around a flexible conductive core. Isaac removed the finished pieces while they were still warm and arranged them across the floor. The companion rotated a model above the chamber, changing proportions whenever Isaac looked away.

“You added claws.”

“Grip supports.”

“They are claws.”

“They can be two things.”

The arms followed. Then the legs, each one built from narrow chromatic plates over a dark mechanical frame. The torso took three attempts because the first version could not support the shoulder assembly and the second had nowhere to house the power cell.

By the third hour, the floor had disappeared beneath tools, rejected joints and strips of unused filament. Isaac burned his thumb against a connector. Pain flashed up his hand and faded almost immediately. A silver line formed beneath the skin, closed the damaged tissue and vanished.

The wall displayed a medical notice. Minor thermal injury corrected. No clinical action required. The companion watched from above the printer.

“You all right?”

“The room says I am.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

Isaac flexed his hand. The skin had already returned to normal.

“I’m fine.”

They continued. Near dawn, the unfinished body sat upright against the sofa. It had two arms, two legs and a torso shaped more for movement than appearance. Its polished plates reflected the artificial galaxy overhead. There was no head. The control socket stayed open at the base of the neck. Isaac lifted the link node.

“You’re sure about this?”

“No.”

He stopped. The companion smiled.

“I’ve never had a body before. Being sure would be suspicious.”

Isaac turned the node in his hands. The glass ring had begun to glow.

“What happens to you in the wall?”

“I leave.”

“Your backup?”

“Remains where it is.”

“So this isn’t really all of you.”

The smile weakened, but did not disappear.

“How much of you is inside your medical backup?”

“That’s different.”

“Because you’re accustomed to yours.”

Isaac looked from the node to the open socket.

“Can I remove it?”

“Probably.”

“That isn’t reassuring.”

“It is more reassuring than definitely not.”

He pushed the node into place. For one second, nothing happened. Then every display in the apartment went black. The room stopped humming. The ventilation paused. The hidden kitchen lights went out. Even the artificial sky disappeared, leaving the ceiling pale in the approaching dawn.

A red point appeared inside the glass ring. It changed to amber. Then green. The mechanical hand moved. Its fingers closed one after another, beginning with the smallest. The wrist rotated. Both arms pressed against the sofa, and the body pulled itself upright with a scrape of metal against the floor.

It stood too quickly. One knee bent in the wrong direction. The torso twisted, struck the table and nearly fell. Isaac caught it under one arm.

“Tall was a bad idea.”

A voice emerged from the speaker inside its chest.

“Your support at this historic moment is overwhelming.”

“You don’t have a head.”

“I had noticed.”

The apartment displays returned. The familiar face appeared across the body’s upper chest, stretched slightly by the proportions of the panel. He looked down at his hands. The claws opened. Closed. Opened again. For the first time since Isaac had known him, he reached for something that existed outside a screen. He picked up a discarded connector. The metal slipped between his fingers and struck the floor. He stared at it.

Isaac waited for the joke.

None came. The mechanical body crouched, less awkwardly this time, and tried again. The connector rose between two careful fingers.

“I can feel its weight,” he said.

“Through the frame?”

“Through me.”

The apartment requested registration. A form appeared beside them.

FABRICATED UNIT CATEGORY

Domestic machine. Mobility support. Commercial assistant. Recreational construct. Other. Isaac selected Other. A warning appeared. Additional review may be required. He selected it again. The form asked for the unit’s name.

“Gleebs,” Isaac said.

The word filled the field.

Primary owner?

Isaac hesitated. The figure beside him was still studying the connector.

“Do I put myself?”

“Do you own me?”

“I paid for the materials.”

“I’ll repay you in emotional enrichment.”

“That could take decades.”

The system waited. Isaac left the field blank. A soft tone sounded. Registration incomplete. Unit may operate within the registered residence. Public operation requires classification. The newly made hand reached across him and selected Recreational construct. Isaac looked over.

“You’re all right with that?”

“I would like to go outside before one of us becomes philosophical.”

They made a head from parts available at a repair shop in the Market District. The shop opened early for automated service, and the route required only twelve minutes by foot. Isaac expected the unfinished body to use the residential transit system. It refused.

“I have legs.”

“You’ve had them for eight minutes.”

“Then I am already behind.”

The apartment door opened. The corridor recognized Isaac and illuminated a path toward the lift. Morning light traveled through the building’s transparent outer layer, turning the walls gold. Far below,

the city was waking in clean horizontal bands: gardens unfolding from rooftops, delivery traffic rising through marked air lanes, transit lines glowing beneath the streets. The lift arrived without being called. Inside, a residential counter appeared above the door.

OCCUPANTS DEPARTING: 2

Isaac noticed it and smiled. The number flickered.

RESIDENTS: 1

REGISTERED DEVICES: 1

The doors began to close. The body beside him stayed still.

“Did you see that?” Isaac asked.

“See what?”

The lift descended. Through its glass wall, the island opened beneath them. Buildings rose in white, black and silver layers, their upper surfaces adjusting to the morning sun. Water ran through narrow channels between walking paths and vanished beneath gardens suspended over the avenues. A train passed without sound, visible for a moment through a transparent section of the city before disappearing into the structures below.

Above everything, the public sky was changing.

The stars faded. Saturn remained visible in daylight for several seconds longer than nature allowed, its rings stretching across the blue before the city removed them. At street level, the door opened onto warm air and the smell of rain from a weather cycle that had ended before Isaac woke. The new body took one step beyond the building. The pavement lit beneath its foot. A maintenance camera turned toward them. There was a pause—small enough to be mistaken for ordinary processing.

Then the camera followed.
